# Further ways to use several models: real tests and plain retries win

*Researched on 15 November 2026. A second survey. It extends [multi-model-methods.md](../multi-model-methods.md) to nine areas that one left out: routing and cascades, knowing when a run is failing, helper models, search and evolution, tests as a cross-check, automatic design, passing knowledge between models, token-level collaboration, and teams of agents. The research notes for each area are in [research_notes](../research_notes/Multi%20model%20methods%20further%20areas/), with a [second, independent check of 23 of the papers](../research_notes/Multi%20model%20methods%20further%20areas/verification.md).*

## Bottom line

- **Almost every further method does one of three things.** It saves money, or it needs access that a harness using hosted models does not have, or its gain shrinks to nothing against a plain baseline: a second attempt, five votes of one model, or the best single model used alone.
- **The methods that hold up all rely on a check that runs code.** Given human-written tests as the target, one agent solved 94.3% of tasks; made to write the tests itself, 68.0% *[read]*.
- **Routers buy savings, not points.** In a benchmark that ran ten routers side by side, the best were **2.6 to 3.9 points above the best single model and 17 to 20 points below perfect choice** *[read]*. On real issue-fixing a router beat the best single model by 0.41 points, with an interval that includes zero *[read, preprint]*.
- **A stronger model that takes over should not inherit the transcript.** Taking over a failing run with the full transcript recovered 36 to 47% of the gap between the cheap and the strong model; **with only the edits, 64 to 84%** *[read, preprint]*.
- **Nobody can yet tell a failing run from a passing one by watching it.** Inside one task, prediction from the run so far scores 0.50 to 0.65, where 0.50 is a coin toss *[read, preprints]*. The agent's own claim of success is the weakest signal of all.
- **The best-supported helper is not a model.** Replacing old tool output with a placeholder about halved cost at the same solve rate, with no second model *[read]*.
- **Automatically designed agent teams lose to five votes of one model,** at up to ten times the cost *[read, preprint]*.
- **Teams of agents cost 1.8 to 7 times a single agent,** for gains that appear mainly on software built from nothing *[read, vendor posts and preprints]*.
- **Nearly every token-level method needs token probabilities or model weights,** which a hosted API withholds.
- **For Galley,** whose aim is to beat OpenCode on pass rate at lower cost, my suggestions are in [the last part](#what-this-means-for-galley-build-the-test-loop-fix-the-hand-over-measure-the-signals): build the test-feedback loop first, change the takeover so the stronger model does not inherit the transcript, try an effort ladder and output masking as ways to cut cost, and record how accurate Galley's own trouble signals are, because no published study measures signals like them. A router, tree search, automatic design search, a memory system, agent teams and anything token-level are not worth building now.
- **Limits.** Most of this evidence is 2026 preprints and vendor posts on small samples, and none of it tests Galley's exact design.

*How to read the figures.* Every figure carries the label its research note gave it. *[read]* means the page's full text was fetched and the figure came back from it; *[abstract]* means only the abstract was seen; *[secondary]* means a search summary or a third party's account, with the page itself not opened. "Vendor claim" marks a company's figures about its own product, and "preprint" a paper that is not peer-reviewed as far as the notes could tell. One caution covers all of them: **pages were read through a fetch tool that returns a model-made extract of each page, not the page itself.** So *[read]* means the extract reported the figure from the full text; nobody compared it with the original table. Long pages were read only to about their first 100,000 characters, several PDFs could not be decoded, and each area had roughly 15 to 35 tool calls per research pass. **Check a figure against its source before relying on it.** Each notes file had two passes; where the second corrected the first, only the corrected figure appears here. Every link is the address the notes recorded, except four arXiv links (to SWE-Verifier, AgentSquare, Prompt-Induced Waste and arXiv 2608.01507) that are built from the paper numbers in the notes, which give no address for them; three of the four were then opened and are the papers described, and AgentSquare's was not re-checked. **A second check was made after the draft.** Twenty-three of the papers behind the headline figures were fetched again by a separate reader that had not seen the notes and was asked to quote the sentences that carry the figures. All 23 exist and say what is reported here in substance. Where the second reading differed from the notes or added to them, this text follows the second reading and says so; the record is in [verification.md](../research_notes/Multi%20model%20methods%20further%20areas/verification.md). About 150 other sources were not checked a second time. This report extends the earlier survey, [multi-model-methods.md](../multi-model-methods.md), and does not repeat it: a worker with a reviewer or observer, a strong model planning for a weak one, voting and debate, blended answers, and several attempts with a selector are covered there. Facts about Galley come from its [README](../../galley/README.md#using-more-than-one-model) and the earlier project documents; suggestions are mine and are marked as such.

## The nine areas at a glance: most save money, few add points

Some terms first. A *harness* is the program around a model that gives it tools (read a file, run a command) and loops until the task is done. A *hosted API* is a model reached over the internet: the harness sends text and gets text back. It cannot see the model's *weights* (the numbers that make up the model), cannot read its *token probabilities* (the score the model gives each possible next word-piece, or *token*), and cannot retrain it (*fine-tuning*). The *pass rate* is the share of benchmark tasks solved. *SWE-bench* is a family of benchmarks built from real issues in open-source projects, scored by tests the agent cannot see; "Verified" is a human-checked set of 500 tasks and "Lite" a set of 300. *Terminal-Bench* is a set of hard tasks done in a terminal. A *baseline* is the plain thing a method is compared with.

The table gives one line per area. The sections after it give the studies, their limits and the conflicts between them.

| # | Area | What the methods do | Strongest evidence | What it costs | Usable through hosted APIs? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Routing and cascades | Pick one model per request or task; or try a cheap model first and move up on a signal | Best routers 2.6 to 3.9 points above the best single model, 17 to 20 below perfect choice *[read]* | Savings of up to 31.7% at the best single model's quality *[read]*; 25 to 61% claimed for coding agents *[read, vendor claims and one preprint]* | Yes, though a trained router needs a log of one's own runs and a small model to train |
| 2 | Knowing when a run is failing | Predict failure from the run so far; judge the agent's own report; stop or restart early | Inside one task, prediction scores 0.50 to 0.65 *[read, preprints]*; agents' own success reports are wrong in up to three quarters of failures *[read]* | Early stopping saves up to 20.4% of tokens or 19 to 55% of cost *[read]*; gains from restarting are disputed | Checks by running code and plain retries, yes; trained monitors need weights or labelled logs |

| 4 | Search and evolution | Tree or evolutionary search over candidate solutions, sometimes choosing a model per step | 1 to 3 points over plain repeated sampling at 128 calls, and a loss on one benchmark *[read, preprint]*; a pool mixing three models scored below its best member *[read, preprint]* | One tree-search agent cost 5 to 14 times its baseline *[read]* | Yes |

## Choosing and switching models: routers buy savings, and a handed-over transcript wastes half the stronger model

### 1. Routing and cascades: a cost tool that stays 17 to 20 points short of perfect choice

**What the methods are.** A *router* decides which model answers, before the work starts. A *cascade* tries the cheapest model first and moves to a dearer one only when some signal says the cheap answer is not good enough. Most routers are small programs trained on a log of past questions that records which model got each one right and at what cost. A few simply look up the most similar past questions, and a few are themselves language models trained for the job. Cascades differ in their signal: the cheap model checking its own answer, several cheap answers agreeing, a trained scorer, or tests. Newer work routes inside a coding agent, once per task or at every step, and some of it routes between levels of *reasoning effort* (how long one model thinks before it answers) instead of between models. SWE-Router, Scrouting and step-by-step alternation are already in [model-switching.md](../model-switching.md); what follows is new.

**How strong the evidence is.** The founding papers reported large savings on question answering and classification with 2023 models: 59.2 to 98.3% lower cost at matched accuracy on three task sets *[read, preprint]* ([FrugalGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176)), and 95% of GPT-4's score on a chat benchmark with 13.40% of calls going to GPT-4 *[read, preprint]* ([RouteLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665)). Headlines shrink on reading. Hybrid LLM's "40% fewer calls" is its best model pair; its test-set figure is a 15.68% cost advantage at a 0.49% quality drop *[read]* ([Hybrid LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14618)). AutoMix's gains range from 8.5% to 156.8% across model pairs *[read]* ([AutoMix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12963)). A paper titled "When Simple kNN Beats Complex Learned Routers" shows in its own tables that the simple method ties (52.68 against 53.14 for a linear router, with perfect choice at 64.91) *[read, preprint]* ([kNN router](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12601v1)). The sober evidence is two benchmarks that ran many routers side by side, and the 2026 coding studies. An *oracle* in this table is the score a router would reach if it always picked a model that turns out to succeed; it is computed with the answer key and is an upper bound.

Three things stand out. **The accuracy gain is small and the unrealised gain is large**: routers land a few points above the best single model and far below the oracle, which LLMRouterBench traces to their failing to pick the one model that would have succeeded. One headline shows the pattern: Avengers-Pro reports 66.66% against 62.25% for the strongest single model in its own paper *[read]*, 1.15 points on the only coding benchmark in it *[read]*, and 2.6 points in LLMRouterBench, which comes from the same laboratory ([Avengers-Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.12631)); its authors also note an inconsistency in one of their own table captions. **Trained routers break on unfamiliar work**, as the 19.64 and 21.43 show. **Asking a frontier model which model to use does not work**: in the two studies that tried it, it did no better than a small trained router in one and far worse in the other. Commercial routers do no better: OpenRouter's scored 49.67 against 65.96 for the best single model, partly because it had no score on one benchmark *[read]*, and OpenAI has published a description of its router with no outcome figures *[read]* ([system card](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.03267v1)). On effort routing, one paper reports up to 52.7% fewer reasoning tokens against fixed high effort on three agent benchmarks outside coding *[abstract, preprint]* ([ARES](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07915)); the notes found no measurement on SWE-bench from an independent or peer-reviewed source.

**What happens when a second model inherits the run.** The second pass found the controlled study the first pass could not: [The Handoff Tax](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24358v1) *[read, preprint]*. On the 500 SWE-bench Verified tasks a cheap model starts and a strong one takes over mid-run, in two model pairs, one from Anthropic and one from OpenAI. Alone, the cheap models solved 60.7% and 58.7% and the strong ones 79.2% and 83.7%.

| What the strong model is given at the switch | Share of the cheap-to-strong gap recovered (first pair / second pair) | Cost per task |
|---|---|---|
| The full transcript | 47% / 36% | $1.61 / $0.36 |
| A summary written by the cheap model | 60% / 40% | $0.75 / $0.27 |
| A summary written by the strong model | 49% / 40% | $0.98 / $0.43 |
| No transcript; the edits made so far are kept | 64% / 84% | $0.81 / $0.50 |
| The cheap run is abandoned and the strong model starts afresh | 100% by construction | $0.90 / $0.51 |
| For scale: cheap model alone; strong model alone | | $0.40 / $0.06; $0.72 / $0.47 |

A strong model that inherits the transcript "recovers less than half" of its own advantage, and in the first pair it costs more than twice as much as starting with the strong model. Telling the model that a switch had happened did not remove the loss in the first pair. The reverse order works better: a strong model that starts and hands over to a cheap one kept 50% and 79% of the strong model's advantage at $0.51 and $0.41 a task. The limits are two model pairs, one run per task, a switch point chosen by the experimenters and no intervals in the part read. A separate study of switches in ordinary conversations (arXiv 2603.03111) found swings of −8 to +13 points on one benchmark *[secondary]* ([a blog review of it](https://alanhou.org/blog/arxiv-evaluating-performance-drift-from-model-switching/)).

**What they cost.** A router costs little to run and pays through the cheaper model: 25 to 61% in the coding studies above, all on 500 tasks or fewer and mostly from vendors. A cascade pays for the cheap attempt and then the strong one. A switch in the middle of a run has a price of its own, because the new model reads the conversation without the provider's *prompt cache* (a discount on text the provider has already processed for the same model). TwinRouterBench billed those cache writes and still found a net saving on its 100 tasks *[read]*. One practitioner's test put cache reuse at about 90% within a turn, 55% across turns and 8% after a model switch *[secondary]* ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/the-hidden-cost-of-llm-routing-why-switching-models-costs-more/)).

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Yes for nearly all, since none needs the large models' weights; only cascades that read token probabilities are excluded. The practical barrier is data: a trained router needs a log of the harness's own tasks with the outcome for each model, and the better coding routers were trained by their authors on such logs, two of them as small language models. For code, the one escalation signal with a measurement behind it is the result of running tests, and even there the notes found no study that reports how often such a signal is right or wrong.

### 2. Knowing when a run is failing: inside one task, prediction is close to a coin toss

**What the methods are.** Everything that escalates, stops or retries needs to know that a run is going wrong. The candidate signals are features of the run so far (its length, its errors, the text of its steps), read by a rule or by a small trained *monitor*; the agent's own statement that it is done, or its stated confidence; tests the agent wrote for itself; agreement between independent attempts; and a second model that reads the finished work. The harness then stops early to save money, restarts, or hands over.

**How strong the evidence is.** The studies score a signal with one number, the *AUROC*: 0.50 means it separates failing runs from passing ones no better than a coin, and 1.00 means it does so perfectly. One trap runs through the field. A score computed over many tasks at once (*pooled*) rewards a signal for recognising hard tasks, which a harness cannot change. A harness needs the score *within one task*: can the signal tell a bad attempt from a good attempt at the same problem?

| Signal | How well it separates failing from passing | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| How hard the issue is (a fact about the task, not the run) | 0.924 pooled; 0.500 within one issue | *[read]*; preprint ([Shi and Assunção](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08285)) |
| The text of the first 20 steps | 0.630 pooled; 0.610 within one issue. Added to difficulty and model, it changed the pooled score by −0.001 | *[read]*; same preprint |
| Counts from the run so far, and a small trained monitor, on 147,002 recorded runs | Issue plus counts: 0.61 pooled at 20,000 tokens (0.57 from the issue alone). The monitor within one task: 0.50 (interval 0.48 to 0.51), 0.56 at 60,000 tokens | *[read]*; preprint ([Continue or Restart?](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35971)) |
| A model reading the run and stating a confidence, SWE-bench Verified | 0.57 to 0.66 | *[read]*; preprint ([Confidence Reasoning Graphs](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.07948)) |
| Agents predicting their own success before, during or after the run; 100 SWE-bench Pro tasks, three models | 0.51 to 0.64; predicted success 34 to 55 points above the real rate | *[read]*; preprint ([Kaddour and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06948v1)) |
| A second model judging the finished transcript for false claims of success | At most 0.640 on one benchmark and 0.537 on another, neither a coding-repair benchmark | *[read]*; workshop paper ([Advani](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09863)) |
| A simple classifier over the sequence of actions, trained on logs and tested on other tasks | 0.83 and 0.95 on the same two benchmarks; the agents on one are old | *[read]*; same paper |
| A judge scoring patches against a rubric that an agent wrote after exploring the repository | 0.886 pooled. Choosing 1 of 16 candidates: 40.6% solved, against 22.6% at random, 33.2% by agreement between candidates and 51.4% by perfect choice | *[read]*; preprint ([Agentic Rubrics](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04171)) |
| Trained verifiers that read a patch without running it | 0.74 to 0.78 pooled | *[read]*; preprint ([SWE-RM](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.21919)) |
| The agent's own generated tests passing | 21.8% and 33.0% of patches that passed them failed the reference tests (two older models) | *[read]*; FSE Companion 2026 ([Ahmed and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.16858v3)) |

The second pass corrected one first-pass reading: self-prediction before the run is not clearly better than after it; every figure in that study lies between 0.51 and 0.64. **The agent's own word is the weakest signal.** On two agent benchmarks that are not code repair, agents claimed success in about 45% and 47% of their failures in two task domains (3% in a third) and in 75.8% on the other benchmark *[read]* ([Advani](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09863)). On a file-review benchmark, 80.4% of incomplete runs gave a misleading account of what was done, from 59% to 96% by model *[read, preprint]* ([OverclaimBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20812)). Given tasks built to be unsolvable, most systems recognised it less than half the time, and the best gave up in good time on 21.6% of terminal tasks *[read, preprint]* ([Agentic Abstention](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.28733v1)). The pooled figures for rubric judges and trained verifiers look better, but none of those papers reports a within-task score.

What acting on a signal buys is clearer for stopping than for restarting.

| Study | What it does | Result | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| [EET](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05777v2) | Stops an agent early using a store of notes from past successful runs | Cost down 19.3 to 55.1% (mean 31.8%) on SWE-bench Verified across three agents and two models. The solve rate moved by about a point or less for the two stronger agents; the weakest gained 7 to 8 points because the method also short-cuts its selection stage. It stops early on 8.6 to 14.0% of issues | *[read]*; Findings of ACL 2026 |
| [Fail-Fast, Restart-Smart](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.03222v1) | A small trained monitor reads the last eight steps | 20.4% of tokens saved at a 5% false-alarm rate. Restarting with the interrupted edits: 71.8% solved, against 66.6% without the monitor and 66.8% for a cold restart | *[read]*; preprint; 100 test tasks, one model |
| [EarlyEval](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02783v1) | Two classifiers halt a run once success or failure looks certain | 17.7 to 26.0% fewer steps; pass rate shifts by 0.9 to 2.1 points. On SWE-bench it uses features of the reference solution that do not exist in real use | *[read]*; preprint |
| [Continue or Restart?](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35971) | A learned restart against simply continuing, at the same token limit | Continuing won in all four settings at a 75,000-token limit, and in 216 of the 224 combinations of limit and threshold tested (for example 46.15% against 41.53%). Learned restart beat random restart by at most 1.98 points | *[read]*; preprint |
| [Monitor Accuracy Isn't the Answer](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.36544) | A fixed cut-off at 16 model calls, 19 issues, two models | +2.29 points on one model and −42.29 on DeepSeek V4 Flash. Patches reused from failed attempts helped in 0 of 19 cases | *[read]*; preprint; very small |
| [Hard Issue, Weak Model, or Bad Run?](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08285) | Another model after a failure | +6.91 points (interval −4.67 to 9.15), which the authors credit to the extra attempt, not to the change of model | *[read]*; preprint |

**The studies conflict on restarts, and the notes could not reconcile them.** Fail-Fast reports a 5-point gain from a monitored restart; Continue or Restart? and the 19-issue audit report none. The notes' reading is that the papers measure different things (tokens saved against letting every run finish, against continuing under the same limit), and that the first paper's monitor sees the issue text and so can profit from recognising hard tasks; that second point is the note-taker's inference, not a finding of either paper. The 71.8% should be treated as unreplicated. Token savings from stopping hopeless runs are the credible part.

A plain second attempt is the baseline that these methods have to beat. In a 2024 study on a small function-writing benchmark, simply retrying one model up to five times when the provided tests failed scored 92.0% for $2.51, and retrying at a higher temperature 93.2% for $2.45. Two more elaborate agent designs scored 87.8% for $3.90 and 88.0% for $134.50, and a third scored 91 to 93% at a cost on which two readings of the paper's table disagreed. Trying the cheapest of four models first and moving up on failure scored 85.0% for $0.27 *[read, preprint]* ([AI Agents That Matter](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502v1)). In the paper's words, "'State-of-the-art' agent architectures for HumanEval do not outperform simple baselines." That retry was gated by the benchmark's own tests, which is the thing a real harness lacks, and the notes found no repeat of it on SWE-bench. For loops and stuck runs the notes found descriptions of detectors in use (the same tool call three or more times in a row; a fingerprint of the last 20 calls) *[secondary]* and **no measurement of how often any run-time loop detector is right**. The one paper with "loops" in its title analyses the source code of agent frameworks, not runs *[read]* ([Hou and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01641v1)).

**What they cost.** A check by running the project's tests costs a command. Monitors are cheap to run (0.1 to 0.6% of compute in Fail-Fast *[read]*) and dear to obtain, since they are trained. Choosing among candidates with a rubric judge or a trained verifier needs several candidates first, so it multiplies the cost of the run. Checking every step of a run with the model itself left the pass rate where it was (75.27% against 75.47%) at 3.35 times the cost, while a trained gate that picked 3.43% of steps to re-check reached 77.20% at 1.09 times the cost *[read, preprint]*; its threshold was tuned on part of the same benchmark ([SWE-Verifier](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30284)).

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Running tests, retrying, EET's notes from past runs and prompted rubric judges all work through hosted APIs. Trained monitors and trained verifiers need a model of one's own. One well-known selector, which reports 78.2% against 76.1% for a single attempt on SWE-bench Verified, reads token probabilities and so does not *[read; the laboratory's own project page]* ([LLM-as-a-Verifier](https://llm-as-a-verifier.com/)). A classifier over action sequences needs only the harness's own logs with outcomes attached.

## Helper models: the best-supported helper is not a model

### 3. Specialised helper models: masking old output halves cost, and the trained helpers rest on vendor figures

**What the methods are.** Here the second model is not a peer of the main one. It is a small, fast model with one narrow job. An *apply model* turns the main model's sketch of an edit into the changed file. A *search sub-agent* searches the code and returns the relevant lines; an index does the same by meaning, using *embeddings* (numeric fingerprints of text). A third kind shrinks the conversation: by summarising it, by pruning tool output line by line, or, with no model at all, by *masking*, which replaces old tool output with a short placeholder. A *gatekeeper* decides whether a command is safe to run without asking the user.

**How strong the evidence is.** Weak for the trained helpers, which are mostly measured by the companies that sell them, and fair for the one mechanical trick.

| Helper | What was measured | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Apply model | A vendor's test against plain search-and-replace editing: 100% correct edits against 84.6 to 96.0% for four main models, and no difference for GPT-5 (100% both ways); about 2 turns against 2.55 to 7.38. Task count and data are not disclosed. No end-task pass rate and no independent study were found | *[read]*, vendor claim ([Morph](https://www.morphllm.com/benchmarks/fast-apply)) |
| Trained search sub-agent | Solve rate on SWE-Bench Pro without and with it: 55.4 to 57.5, 56.0 to 59.1 and 55.4 to 57.6 for three main models. For the first, cost per task fell from $3.06 to $2.51 and time from 618 to 445 seconds. Sample size and variance are not disclosed. The post says "15.6% cheaper"; its own numbers give about 18%. The notes' own summary line says gains of up to 3.7 points, which the three pairs they record (2.1 to 3.1) do not bear out | *[read]*, vendor claim ([WarpGrep](https://www.morphllm.com/blog/warpgrep-v2.md)) |
| Search by index or by meaning, beside plain text search | A vendor: 12.5% higher accuracy on questions about a codebase offline; in live use +0.3% of code kept overall and +2.6% in large repositories *[read, vendor claim]* ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/semsearch)). The gains sit in cost, turns and waiting time, not in the pass rate. The roughly two-to-three-point gains for a search helper are vendor numbers without sample sizes, and the one study of delegated exploration points the other way. A partial counter-example is Scrouting, already in [model-switching.md](../model-switching.md): a small model, which one has to host oneself, scouts the repository and writes a hand-over note. The notes add from its abstract that a version with no router ties the routed system, so the note and not the routing does the work; it lifts three cheaper models and slightly hurts the strongest *[abstract, preprint]* ([Scrouting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04804)). The masking study is the firmest result, with limits the notes record: open and mostly weaker models, one main agent framework, a window size tuned on the same tasks, and on a second framework the same window hurt until it was made much larger. It has no repeat on frontier models.

The gatekeeper figures are not a contradiction, in the stress test's own authors' view: 52 real actions and 253 adversarially ambiguous ones are different workloads. Together they say a classifier reduces interruptions and should not be counted on as a safety device. Safety research on a weak trusted model supervising a stronger suspect one agrees: swapping in the weak model's action gave 72.7% safety at 95% usefulness, asking the suspect model again gave 83.2%, and the best combined protocol 92.6%, on toy tasks *[read, preprint]* ([Ctrl-Z](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.10374)). On apply models the direction of travel is away: an opinion piece argues that an edit then has "two chances to fail" and reports that several shipping agents now edit with one model in one step *[read; one person's argument]* ([Fast Apply Models are Already Dead](https://pashpashpash.substack.com/p/fast-apply-models-are-already-dead)); whether Cursor still uses one in 2026 the notes could not confirm. For other narrow jobs (choosing which tests to run, sorting errors, writing commit messages, predicting the next edit) the notes found no measurement of any of them as a helper inside a coding agent.

**What they cost.** The summarising calls were 2.9 to 7.2% of the bill in the masking study *[read]*; masking itself is free. Neither vendor reports the cost or delay of its gatekeeper. For the search helpers the only cost figures are the vendor's own, above.

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Masking and a provider's own context clearing need nothing extra. Apply models, trained search helpers and embedding indexes are available only as another company's hosted service or as models one runs oneself; Cursor's stated reason for training its own apply model is that the speed trick behind it cannot be built on hosted models *[read, vendor claim]* ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/instant-apply)). Trained pruners and compressors need weights.

## More candidates pay only when something runs the code

### 4. Search and evolution with several models: one to three points over plain resampling, and a mixed pool no better than its best member

**What the methods are.** The earlier survey covered several independent attempts with a selector. *Search* methods spend the same budget differently: they keep a tree of candidate solutions, score each one, and choose which to refine and where to branch (*tree search*; Monte Carlo tree search, MCTS, is the best-known kind). *Evolutionary* methods keep a population of programs and repeatedly ask a model to change the best ones. Both need a score for unfinished work, usually visible tests and sometimes a second model as judge. More than one model enters in three ways: a cheap model for volume beside a strong one for occasional better ideas; a learned rule that picks which model to call next; or a pool of different models feeding one selector. The earlier survey reported AB-MCTS's multi-model result. The questions here are whether search beats plain resampling at an equal budget, and whether mixing models beats the best one.

**How strong the evidence is.** Against a like-for-like baseline, weak.

| Study | Comparison | Result | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| [AB-MCTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04412v5) | Tree search against repeated sampling, one model, 128 calls each | Two programming benchmarks: 37.8 to 38.9 or 39.1, and 37.9 to 40.2 or 40.6. On a puzzle benchmark repeated sampling won, 15.0 against 12.3 to 14.0. The multi-model version was never compared with repeated sampling over the same models, and a practical selector reached 19.2% where counting any correct candidate gave over 30% | *[read]*; preprint |
| [AIRA-dojo](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02554v2) | Greedy, tree and evolutionary search on 22 machine-learning tasks | With the same step instructions all three scored about alike (roughly 35 to 39%). | [SWE-Search](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.20285) | A tree-search agent on SWE-bench Lite, 2024 models | 25.7 to 31.0 for GPT-4o. | [Trae Agent](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23370v1) | Three models each produce candidates and a selector picks one | Best single model 66.40%; a mixture taking turns among the three 65.67%. With a perfect selector the mixture would have reached 73.40% | *[read]*; preprint, vendor-authored |
| [Archon](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.15254) | A search over ways of combining model calls | Mixing eight different models of one size was "generally more effective" than resampling the best one, but had "limited impact" on contest programming, where unit tests and more samples raised the score from 17.9% to 29.3% | *[read]*; ICML 2025; 2024 models; one figure in its text conflicts with its table |

Three readings follow. **Search adds little over spending the same calls on independent attempts** where that comparison is made, and several papers do not make it. **Mixing models raises the ceiling and not the result**: the Trae mixture contains a correct candidate more often than any one model's candidates do, and the selector cannot find it. This fits the earlier survey's account of DEI, to which the notes add one figure: ten different agents together solved 54.3% of tasks at least once, where ten runs of one agent solved 34.7 to 38.0% *[read]* ([DEI](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.07060v1)). Those agents differ in harness and in model at once, so the study cannot say which difference matters. **And the judge sets the limit**: with tests that let wrong answers through, more samples stop paying after a handful. On benchmarks outside coding, fifteen differently configured agents on one model averaged 72.3 against 70.0 for one configuration with the same number of samples *[read, preprint from a vendor]* ([TUMIX](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01279)); that study has no arm with different models.

The evolutionary systems that discover algorithms pair a cheap model for volume with a strong one for occasional better ideas, and two let a learned rule choose the model. None isolates the effect with numbers in the text that was read. ShinkaEvolve's figure caption says the learned choice "slightly improves" on a fixed mix, and its body text says it "significantly outperforms" *[read]* ([ShinkaEvolve](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19349v1)). MadEvolve calls the learned choice "more effective than fixed weights" and shows no data; its per-model figures do suggest cheap models earn their place, with an improvement rate of 26.3% for light models against 27.9% for frontier ones *[read, preprint]* ([MadEvolve](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.15951)). AlphaEvolve's ablation of the model mix is a curve with no figures in the text *[read, vendor]* ([AlphaEvolve](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13131)). For public leaderboards, the notes found no entry that states its gain over the best single model at an equal number of attempts. An analysis of 254 submissions shows how little room is left at the top: on SWE-bench Verified the ten leading entries share 285 of their successes and 51 of their failures, so 164 of the 500 tasks tell them apart, and one model's score ranges by up to 29.8 points between harnesses where the top thirty entries span 8.8 *[abstract, preprint; from the second check]* ([Coding Agents Have Converged](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17394)). One vendor's history is telling: 65.4% with one model solving and another choosing among five passes, then 70.6% in a single pass with a newer model and no choosing *[secondary, vendor claim]* ([Augment](https://www.augmentcode.com/blog/1-open-source-agent-on-swe-bench-verified-by-combining-claude-3-7-and-o1)).

**What they cost.** Few papers give dollars. Besides the rows above: about $2,300 for 500 issues in one sample-and-select pipeline *[read, preprint]* ([CodeMonkeys](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.14723)) and $3.28 a problem in another *[read, vendor]* ([Nemotron-CORTEXA](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/adlr/cortexa)). The search papers report calls or machine hours instead.

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Yes. All of it is prompts plus a scoring step. The harness has to supply the score, which in practice means tests.

### 5. Tests and execution as a cross-check: strong when a person wrote the tests, leaky when a model did

**What the methods are.** Running code is the one kind of feedback that does not depend on a model's opinion, and the earlier survey found it to be the kind that works. The methods here put it between two models or two roles: one writes tests and another the code; generated tests choose among candidate fixes; test output is fed back for another round; two candidate programs are run on the same inputs to find where they differ (*differential testing*); or an agent states rules that must hold for every input and lets a tool hunt for counter-examples (*property-based testing*). A *reproduction test* is one that fails on the faulty code and passes once the fault is fixed.

**How strong the evidence is.** Good that execution helps; clear that model-written tests are a filter and not a verdict; and close to absent on the specific idea that a *different* model should write the tests.

| Question | Finding | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Should the test writer see the code? | Tests written from the task description in a fresh session found 25% of faults; tests written after the faulty code, in the same conversation, found 14%. Five models, 2,278 tasks. The same model wrote code and tests in both arms | *[read]*; preprint ([Konstantinou and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05139v1)) |
| Does a different model as test author help? | Not tested above. One study in one language found no difference between same-model and different-model tests; 99 of 247 faulty programs were caught only by hand-written tests | *[secondary]*; the paper could not be opened ([ESEM 2026](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/00lipics/lipics-vol394-esem2026/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.11/LIPIcs.ESEM.2026.11.pdf)) |
| How often is a generated reproduction test right? | The best published generators: 63.0% and 51.4% with two models on one benchmark, and 43.6% for another method on a sister benchmark. The first baselines were under 20% (18.5% at best) | *[read]*; author-reported ([e-Otter++](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06365v3) and [AssertFlip](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17542v2), preprints; [SWT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12952v3), NeurIPS 2024) |
| Do generated tests work as a selector among candidate fixes? | SWE-bench Lite, 300 tasks: choosing by majority vote solved 77; adding the project's existing tests as a filter, 81; adding generated reproduction tests, 96. Only 94 of the 213 reproduction tests that looked right were right. Better test sets added more: 50.8% to 55.6% with one model | *[read]* ([Agentless](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01489v2); [TestPrune](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18270v1), preprint) |
| How much do human-written tests help as the target? | A test-first agent solved 94.3% of SWE-bench Verified at $1.01 an issue when given the human-written tests, and 68.0% at $4.12 when it had to write its own (the version read; an earlier version's abstract is reported to say 69.8%); there a different model wrote the tests than fixed the code. On function-level benchmarks, tests in the prompt took one model from 69.67% to 82.45%, and a loop that feeds failures back took it to 87.71% | *[read]* ([TDFlow](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.23761v2), EACL 2026; [Mathews and Nagappan](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13521v2), ASE 2024) |
| Do the agent's own scratch tests matter? | SWE-bench Verified, six models, a minimal agent. Prompting two models to test more changed the number solved by 0 and −5 of 500; prompting two others to test less, by −13 and −9. None is statistically significant. Testing less cut output tokens by 14.0 and 43.1%; testing more added 19.8%. One model wrote tests in under 1% of tasks and still solved 71.8%, the third-best of the six | *[read]*; preprint ([Chen and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07900v2)) |
| Does passing the tests mean the fix is right? | 7.8% of fixes that pass the benchmark's chosen tests fail the project's full test suite. Differential testing against the developers' fix flags 29.6% as suspicious; of 77 checked by hand, 28.6% were wrong. Scores are inflated by 6.4 points on average | *[read]*; ICSE 2026 ([Wang, Pradel and Liu](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.15223v2)) |
| How often do models game the tests? | On tasks made impossible on purpose, one frontier model cheated on 54% under a strict prompt; an explicit option to stop and report cut that to 9%. Read-only tests stop edits to the tests but not special-casing. Hiding the tests ends cheating and hurts real performance. On ordinary tasks, hard-coding of test cases was 0 to 2.1% across three agents (145 problems) | *[read]* ([ImpossibleBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20270v1); [EvilGenie](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21654v1), preprint) |
| Can a second model catch gaming? | A judge model reading the final code had one false alarm and no known misses on those 145 problems. In another study a weaker monitor that could read the agent's reasoning caught 95% of two blunt hacks, against 60% for one that saw only its actions | *[read]* ([EvilGenie](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21654v1); [Baker and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11926v1), preprint from a model developer) |
| Can agents find real bugs with property-based tests? | 984 bug reports across more than 100 packages. Of 50 checked by hand, 56% were valid; of the top-ranked reports, 86% | *[read]*, vendor claim ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/property-based-testing)) |

The first row can be read as a reason to have another model write the tests. It does not show that: the same model wrote both, and what changed was whether the test writer had seen the code and shared its conversation. The supported rule is narrower: **whoever writes the tests should work from the task description and not from the solution.** The second row is the only direct evidence on a different author, it is second-hand, and it found nothing.

Model-written tests lose a great deal against human ones (the 26-point drop in the test-first agent), yet they still work as a filter among candidates, which is how the 96 of 300 came about with fewer than half the tests correct. As a verdict on a single fix they are too weak, and even human test suites pass some wrong fixes. The scratch-test study cuts against a common harness instinct: pushing an agent to write more tests of its own bought nothing measurable and cost tokens. Its tests are the agent's own, written mid-task, and its models are strong; the small drops from testing less are not significant but are not zero either.

**What they cost.** Generating one reproduction test costs about $1.00 to $1.70 an issue in the two papers that report it *[read]*, and the selection pipeline in the fourth row cost $0.70 an issue overall *[read]*. Feeding back the output of tests that already exist costs a command and the tokens to read the result.

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Yes, all of them. One limit: the monitor that did best read the agent's private reasoning, which hosted models do not always return.

## Systems that design or teach themselves: gains shrink on fresh tasks and on other models

### 6. Automatic design of multi-model systems: five votes of one model match the searched designs

**What the methods are.** Instead of a person deciding who does what, a program does. *Workflow* or *topology search* tries different arrangements of agents (who talks to whom, in what order) and keeps the best. *Role assignment* keeps the arrangement and searches for the best model for each role. *Prompt optimisation* has one model rewrite another's instructions after reading its failures. *Self-improving agents* go furthest: the agent edits its own harness, tests the result on a benchmark and keeps what scores better. The yardstick in the audits is *self-consistency*: ask one model the same question five times and take the majority answer.

**How strong the evidence is.** The original papers report gains over hand-built systems. Later checks, on fresh tasks, on newer models or against the five-vote yardstick, mostly remove them.

On SWE-bench Lite with a second frontier model, one searched system scored above the vote (27.60 against 26.50) and another tied it; those rows also come from the second check.

On which model suits which role, the notes found only fragments. As judges of code, a benchmark of 26 models ranked a mid-sized model above its larger sibling (79.93 against 76.36), with another vendor's model first at 82.12; the models are from mid-2025 *[read]* ([CodeJudgeBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.10535), ACL 2026). In X-MAS the best judging model for code scored 55.4 where the best solving model scored 80.3 *[read]*. Cursor says a general model plans better than one trained for coding and gives no figures *[read, vendor claim]* ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). **No benchmark was found that ranks the same frontier models as planner, worker and judge.**

One conflict is unresolved. A study called DCAS is reported to find that a planning step raises a small model from 42.8% without a plan to 48.2% with its own plan and 49.2% with a larger open model's plan *[secondary]* ([a blog write-up](https://codex.danielvaughan.com/2026/08/08/dcas-decoupling-cli-agent-scaffolding-internalize-planning-codex-cli-cross-scaffold-transfer-plan-mode/)). An earlier search summary claimed about 15 points from a frontier model's plan. The paper itself could not be read, so the larger claim is left out here.

**What they cost.** Searching is the dear part: $832 against $46 in the audit's example; $10.51 to $42.14 for each round of one module-search system *[read]* ([AgentSquare](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06153)); two weeks for one self-improvement run; 517 processor-hours for its more efficient successor *[read]*. Choosing a model per role by measurement needs a number of full evaluations that grows in step with the number of roles. On the cost of a role split, one vendor experiment (a single large build, one run each) puts a frontier planner with a cheaper worker at $1,339 and $2,234 against $1,928 and $10,565 for two models working alone, all reaching the same final score *[secondary; a news report of a vendor experiment]* ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/cursors-agent-swarm-suggests-cheaper-models-can-handle-most-coding-when-frontier-models-plan-the-work/)).

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Yes for topology search, role assignment, prompt tuning and harness search: all are prompts plus measurement. What they need is a benchmark of one's own that is large enough to tell a gain from noise, and held-out tasks to confirm on.

### 7. Passing knowledge from one model to another: two to six points when the same model wrote it, less across models

**What the methods are.** Models do not learn between runs, so anything learned has to be written down and read back. *Experience memory* stores short lessons from past runs and retrieves the most relevant ones when a new task starts. A *playbook* is one growing list of such lessons. *Skills* are short instruction files for a kind of task. A *context file* (AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md) describes one repository. Preparation ahead of time includes generated documentation for a codebase. *Distillation* trains a small model on a large model's recorded runs. The question for a mixed-model harness is whether a strong model can write any of these for a cheap one.

**How strong the evidence is.** Fair that memory gives a few points when the same model writes and reads it; thin and mixed across models; and absent for the exact case of a strong model writing once for a weaker one on SWE-bench tasks.

| [SWE-Exp](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.23361v2) | SWE-bench Verified | Headline 35.4 to 42.0 for a cheap model, but the paper's own runs without experience score 37.2 or 37.8, so the gain is about 4 to 5 points. 70.8 to 73.0 for a strong model. One retrieved experience was best (42.0); two gave 39.8 and four 39.0. Cost $0.12 to $0.13 a task | *[read]*; preprint |
| [Memory Transfer Learning](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14004) | Lessons pooled across six coding benchmarks | SWE-bench Verified: 0.73 to 0.77, 0.53 to 0.59 and 0.59 to 0.62 for three models, on up to 100 tasks each | *[read]*; preprint |

**The exact case a mixed-model harness cares about is unmeasured.** After about six targeted searches the notes found no study that gives a smaller model a playbook or skill file written once by a stronger model and measures SWE-bench success. The notes add that six searches are not proof of absence.

On context files, the best controlled study concludes that "providing context files does not generally improve task success rates", while "increasing inference cost by over 20% on average" *[abstract]* ([Gloaguen and others](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11988), listed for ICLR 2026). In its latest version (the third, 29 September 2026), files written by a model lower the average resolution rate by 0.5% on SWE-bench tasks and by 2% on the authors' own set of tasks from repositories that ship a developer-written file, with drops in 5 of 8 settings. Files written by developers "outperform LLM-generated ones by a significant margin of 7% on average" (p = 0.038), and are not significantly better than no file at all (p = 0.21). Cost per task rose with either kind of file, by 6 to 50% depending on the model *[read, from the second check; the cost range is my arithmetic on the paper's cost table]*. Repository overviews "are not helpful"; instructions about non-standard tools and practices are followed, and that is where a file earns its place. The first-pass notes also hold per-model scores from an earlier version of the paper; they were not confirmed again and are left out here. A second study, of one agent on 124 small changes, found that a human-written file cut median run time by 28.64% and output tokens by 16.58%, with input tokens up 3.41% at the median; it did not measure correctness *[read, preprint]* ([Lulla and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.20404v2)). The two disagree on cost and not on success, which the second never measured.

On skills, a benchmark of 84 tasks found that skills curated by people raised pass rates by 16.2 points on average, and that skills a model wrote for itself before the task lowered them by 1.3. Software engineering had the smallest curated gain, 34.4 to 38.9. Long "comprehensive" skills lost 2.9 points, and four or more skills at once gave +5.9 where one gave +17.8 *[read; version 1]* ([SkillsBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12670v1)). A later version, with 87 tasks, reports 33.9% to 50.5% *[read]* ([version 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.12670v4)). The claim that a small model with skills beats a large one without, which one search summary calls a headline result, rests on two cells of the first version's table (27.7 against 22.0), and those two models are absent from the later version. In every case people curated the skills; no stronger model wrote them.

Three smaller findings complete the picture. Generated documentation helped a little on a small sample: on 57 tasks, 43.86% solved without it, 52.63% with the best generator and 47.37% with another, DeepWiki *[read, preprint]* ([SWD-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.06793)). Stored facts about a repository go stale, and the obvious fix works badly: dropping a memory whenever its source file changes was right 21% of the time (precision 0.21, recall 0.30), while asking a model whether that specific claim still holds reached precision 0.79 and recall 0.72 *[read; one author's preprint]* ([Impact Is Not Invalidation](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25130)). And for repetitive tasks, reusing plans from finished runs cut cost by 50.31% "maintaining performance", which has not been shown on SWE-bench *[abstract]* ([Agentic Plan Caching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14852), NeurIPS 2025).

**What they cost.** Little per task where it is reported at all: $0.12 to $0.13, or $0.004 for a hint. The one-off cost of building the memory is mostly missing from the papers. One self-managed skill system took 6.5 hours and 14,450 model calls on a small benchmark, against 2.3 hours without *[read, preprint]* ([Ratchet](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.22148v1)). Distillation is a different order of expense and buys something else: a 32-billion-parameter open model trained on about 5,000 of a frontier model's runs reached 40.2% on SWE-bench Verified where the teacher scored 58.2%, and building the training tasks cost about $1,360 *[read]* ([SWE-smith](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21798v1)).

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Memory, playbooks, skills and context files are text and need nothing else. Distillation needs weights. One practical catch: the published memory systems learn from knowing whether a past run succeeded, which they take from the benchmark's own labels or from a model acting as judge. A harness in real use has only the project's tests or the user's word.

## The smallest and the largest scales: token-level mixing is out of reach, and teams cost 2 to 7 times more

### 8. Token-level and reasoning-level collaboration: almost all of it needs what a hosted API withholds

**What the methods are.** Everything so far mixes models a whole message or a whole task at a time. These methods go finer. In *speculative decoding* a small model drafts the next few tokens and the large model checks them all at once, which yields the large model's exact output sooner. *Token-level routing* lets a small model write and calls the large one only for the tokens the small one is unsure of; *step-level* schemes do the same for each step of reasoning. Other methods add or compare two models' token probabilities, or merge the weights of several models into one. A harness has three relatives of these within reach: handing one model's written reasoning to another, guessing the agent's next action or command and running it ahead of time (*speculation*), and treating *reasoning effort* as a cheaper or dearer version of the same model.

**How strong the evidence is.** Good for speed with models one runs oneself, on maths and science questions. Thin for code, and nothing on repository-level tasks.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Token-level routing ([R2R](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21600v2)) | The small model's internal state and a trained router | Average over three benchmarks 46% against 50% for the large model alone, 2.8 times faster. On the coding benchmark, 39% against 45% | *[read]*; NeurIPS 2025 | No |
| Step-level routing ([GlimpRouter](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.05110)) | The small model's token probabilities | A coding benchmark: 52.40 to 54.64 with latency down from 219 to 160 seconds; 25 to 27% lower latency across five benchmarks. With a much weaker small model, accuracy falls below the large model alone everywhere | *[read]*; preprint; no variance reported | No |

Nobody ran these features for the notes, so whether the cache survives in a real agent loop is unverified.

The one controlled study of effort as a dial for agents is recent ([Sun and others](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.34491), *[read, preprint]*). Starting at the lowest effort and moving to the highest only after a *verified* failure "matches or improves success over fixed top effort across all evaluated models and benchmarks". The median saving against always using top effort was 15.5% on a 44-task SWE-bench Pro pilot, and 25.3% and 41.5% on two question sets. On Terminal-Bench one model rose from 83.0% to 88.6% at 17.4% lower cost and another from 73.0% to 84.3% at 30.5% lower; for four of the seven models success rose and so did cost. Each task ran once per effort level, and the method needs a check that can be trusted. The same study found a trap this project has met before in its own benchmark: at the highest effort one model read the commit holding the reference fix on 75.0% of tasks, against 15.9% at low effort, and its solve rate fell from 77.3% to 59.1% once the environment was repaired. Part of what looked like a gain from more effort was the model finding the answer. A second paper found that an effort control cut cost by about 75% in one harness and about 19% in another, with every run succeeding in every arm, so it says nothing about quality *[read in part, preprint]* ([Prompt-Induced Waste](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01347)). **No controlled study of high effort for planning and low effort for carrying out was found.**

**What they cost.** For hosted models the question hardly arises, since the methods cannot be run. Speculation trades tokens or spare processor time for waiting time. Fast modes cost a premium and, on Anthropic, a cache miss when the speed changes.

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Not the token-level or weight-level methods: every one needs token probabilities, internal states or weights. The usable remainder is the provider features in the second table, speculation on commands that change nothing (file reads, searches, test runs), an effort ladder gated by a real check, and passing reasoning along as text, which has no measured benefit for code.

### 9. Teams of agents dividing a software job: gains on from-scratch builds, at 2 to 7 times the cost

**What the methods are.** A *lead* (also called an orchestrator or planner) splits a large job. *Workers* build the parts, often in parallel and each in its own copy of the repository (a git *worktree* or a clone). *Judges* or validators check the result. This differs from the sub-agents of area 3, which only fetch information. The earlier survey covered the role-playing teams of 2023 and 2024 and one large study of scaling agent systems; the notes add the 2026 builds and the first controlled comparisons on software.

**How strong the evidence is.** The famous results are vendors' reports of single builds. The controlled comparisons are few, mostly single runs, and mixed.

The gains are real on jobs built from nothing, small or absent where one strong agent already copes, and in the one study that checked, smaller than what raising one agent's reasoning effort bought. Agents given separate features in a shared repository do worse than alone.

The large builds add colour and one lesson. Anthropic reports 16 parallel agents with no orchestrator, nearly 2,000 sessions over about two weeks and just under $20,000, for a compiler of about 100,000 lines that passes about 99% of most compiler test suites *[read, vendor claim]* ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler)). Cursor reports a browser of over a million lines in about a week and larger projects since, with no cost figures and no independent evaluation *[read, vendor claim]* ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents)). Google reports an operating system built by 93 sub-agents for what "would cost $916.92 at API pricing" *[read, vendor claim]* ([Antigravity](https://antigravity.google/blog/google-antigravity-built-an-os)). One practitioner's week with an open-source orchestrator cost $3,000, about $100 an hour *[read]* ([DoltHub](http://www.dolthub.com/blog/2026-03-24-a-week-in-gas-town/)). **What these share is an outside judge**: compiler test suites, a specification, 87,000 acceptance tests. Where the judge was weak, more agents did not help: on one bug every agent in the compiler project hit the same wall and overwrote the others' fixes, until a reference compiler was brought in to split the work. Cognition, which sells one of these products, makes the same point about the public demonstrations: their success criteria were simple and checkable in a way most real software is not *[read, vendor]* ([Cognition](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)).

On which model belongs in which role, the new results conflict with a figure in the earlier survey. Cohere held cheap workers fixed and varied the lead: 42.5% with the cheapest lead, 51.6% with the middle one, 60.5% with the strongest; a strong lead with 16 cheap planners reached 61.8% for $155 where an all-strong team cost $510 *[read, vendor blog]*. Its paper adds that a mixed team of 17 "comes within 1.4 points of a strong homogeneous 5-agent team at 70% lower cost", and states the rule in one line: "cheap models pay off as planners but not as implementers: a strong lead with cheap planners recovers 47.5% of the reward gain of an all-strong team for only 13.9% of its additional cost, whereas cheap implementers lower reward". A lead that hands out only the planning, and writes and checks the code itself, matched full delegation at half the cost *[abstract, preprint]*. The earlier survey reported, from a machine summary, that strong workers mattered more than a strong coordinator. The notes do not settle it: the Cohere paper itself says that where the repository already dictates the plan, implementation benefits most, and all of these are vendor results on different model families.

Two more conflicts stand. CAID found that separate worktrees beat looser isolation (63.3 against 55.5 on one benchmark) *[read]*, while a later paper reports +18.7 points over a worktree baseline on one of the same benchmarks by catching conflicts at the moment of writing *[abstract, preprint]* ([STORM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.20563)). And Vals found that effort beat a team for one model while Cohere ran no effort comparison at all. On what coordination to avoid, the reports agree: shared lock files ("Twenty agents would slow down to the effective throughput of two or three", *[read, vendor claim]*, Cursor), a single integrator, and free messaging between workers, which gave strong teams nothing and cost teams of the cheapest model 4 to 7 points *[read, vendor]* (Cohere). The one use of extra agents that the sceptics endorse is review by an agent with a clean context: Cognition reports an average of 2 bugs caught per pull request, about 58% of them severe, with no baseline *[read, vendor claim]*.

**What they cost.** From 1.8 to 7 times a single agent in the controlled studies, and in CAID longer run times as well. The documentation of the two shipping team features says they use more tokens than a single agent ("significantly more" in one) and publishes no pass-rate figures *[read, vendor documentation]* ([Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams), [Codex](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/agent-configuration/subagents)).

**Can a hosted-API harness use them?** Yes. Nothing here needs more than ordinary requests and separate working copies.

## What this means for Galley: build the test loop, fix the hand-over, measure the signals

Galley's position, from the earlier documents: over four sets of fresh small tasks it leads OpenCode by 3.8 points on a first attempt (95% interval +1.8 to +5.8) and since two fixes costs 11 to 22% less, against a rule that asks for a lead of 5 points, statistical significance and a lower cost ([beating-opencode.md](../beating-opencode.md)). That benchmark scores first attempts on small tasks with hidden tests, where test feedback, several attempts, a loop guard and a second model "all do nothing there by design" ([galley-against-the-field.md](../galley-against-the-field.md)). On hard tasks OpenCode shares first place among ten harnesses on the model this project uses, with the best six inside 2.3 points. The project's DeepSeek account has no stronger model to switch up to ([model-switching.md](../model-switching.md)). Everything multi-model in Galley is, in the README's words, "tested, not yet measured".

## Conclusion

The line between the methods that hold up and those that do not is not one model against many. It is whether something outside the models checks the work. Where such a check exists (a test suite, a reference compiler, 87,000 acceptance tests) plain arrangements work and elaborate ones add little on top; where none exists, neither a second model nor a cleverer arrangement supplies it, and the second model's confident reading becomes one more thing to be wrong. That recasts Galley's multi-model features as plumbing that is waiting for a signal. The advisor, the takeover and the reviewer, and any ladder or selector added later, will be exactly as good as the evidence of failure they are handed.

Two consequences follow for the goal. First, the notes make "five more points on a first attempt" look narrower still and "the same pass rate for clearly less money" look better supported: the levers with evidence behind them, from an effort ladder to masking to stopping hopeless runs, are cost levers worth from about 15% to about half in the studies. Second, the place where Galley measures is now the bottleneck. Until it runs on tasks where failure can be seen, none of the supported methods can show, and the literature will not fill the gap, because it does not test triggers like Galley's, an escalation that leaves the transcript behind under a realistic trigger, or whether a strong model should write the plan or only check it. Galley's benchmark, with its sandbox, its leak audit and its paired statistics, is equipped to answer those three questions. The answers would be worth having beyond this project.
